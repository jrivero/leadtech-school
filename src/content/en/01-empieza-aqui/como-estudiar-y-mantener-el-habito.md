## Learn by Building, Not Just Watching

Programming combines concepts and skills: you need to understand what a condition means and also practice expressing it, running it, and correcting it. Watching a video or rereading an explanation can help you get oriented, but it does not by itself show that you can use the idea. After studying something, close the material and try to recall it, explain it, or apply it in your own example. If you get stuck, consult the explanation again and note what was missing.

The Cornell Learning Strategies Center describes approaches such as retrieving information from memory, spacing out practice, and alternating related skills. You can adapt those ideas to programming without following a rigid schedule. The important thing is to revisit concepts in more than one session and set aside time to produce something: a prediction, a diagram, a test, or a few lines of code.

## An Example of a Short Study Session

Imagine you want to learn about functions. Instead of writing “study Python” in your planner, define the outcome: “create a function that takes two prices and returns their sum.” The session might last twenty or thirty minutes, or whatever fits your life.

1. **Recall:** For two minutes, write down what you think `def`, parameters, and `return` do without looking at your notes.
2. **Consult:** Read a short explanation and compare it with what you recalled. Mark just one question to investigate.
3. **Build:** Write and run a small function. Predict its output before pressing Run.
4. **Explain:** Add a note to your notebook: “`return` provides the result for another part of the program to use.”
5. **Wrap up:** Write down the next step, for example, “tomorrow I’ll test what happens with zero.”

Start the next session with a brief recall exercise and a different case. A useful schedule might be today, two days from now, and the following week; adjust it to the effort involved and the time you have, rather than treating it as a universal formula.

## Design a Habit That Survives Busy Weeks

Tie studying to a specific cue: after coffee, when you finish work, or at the start of the afternoon. Prepare the file, editor, and a tiny task in advance. Reduce the friction of getting started: “open the project and run the last test” is easier to begin than “master programming.”

Also define a minimum version for difficult days. It might be five minutes spent rereading a question and answering it from memory, fixing an error, or leaving a note for tomorrow. The minimum does not always replace a full session; it maintains continuity and makes it easier to return. Track what you did, not a perfect streak. If you miss a day, resume at the next available opportunity without doubling the workload as punishment.

## Check Whether Your Plan Is Working

At the end of the week, review three pieces of evidence: can you explain an idea without copying the definition? Can you modify an example? What error did you learn to diagnose? If you have logged hours but have no evidence, change the method rather than blaming yourself. Break an oversized task into smaller pieces, find a reference example, or ask for help with a specific question.

If a session drags on without progress, write down the last known state and the exact question. If you forget a concept, that does not mean you are not suited to programming; it means you need to retrieve it again with slightly more guided practice. Alternating reading, recall, and building keeps the session active and makes gaps in your understanding visible.

## Common Mistakes

Avoid vague goals, collecting tutorials without running the code, and measuring progress only by consecutive days. Do not turn a break into a debt, either: you do not have to make up all the missed hours at once. If a goal will not fit into one session, define an intermediate deliverable, such as a function, a test, or a commented example.

## Summary

Choose a small goal, recall before consulting your notes, practice with your own case, and prepare the next step. Space reviews across several days and return without guilt when your routine is interrupted. A useful habit is one you can resume.
