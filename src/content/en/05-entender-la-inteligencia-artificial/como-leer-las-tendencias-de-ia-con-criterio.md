## Core Idea — Conceptual Explanation

AI news mixes research results, internal tests, commercial surveys, and predictions. Before sharing a conclusion, separate four elements: **what is being claimed**, **what was measured**, **on whom or what task**, and **over what period**. “Improved by 20%” does not tell you whether this means percentage points, a relative improvement, a set of questions, or a real-world environment. The number only makes sense alongside its method and comparison.

A benchmark is a test designed to compare systems under certain conditions. It does not automatically represent every task in a profession. It can become outdated if models learn the examples, questions contain errors, or scoring changes. A good report describes the data's limitations and provenance. It is also worth checking who published it and whether they have an incentive to highlight a result. That does not automatically invalidate the information; it shows what you should verify.

Whether a product is still available is another claim to check. A tutorial showing a real interface can become outdated even if its technical explanation remains useful. For example, GitHub announced that GitHub Models would be retired on July 30, 2026; as of this lesson, its catalog and inference are no longer active options. That change does not mean GitHub Copilot has been retired: similar names do not prove that two products are the same. Check the official notice before following old instructions.

Stanford's AI Index brings together data on research, capabilities, and other topics, with different editions and sources. If you cite a figure, keep the year, chapter, and context; do not turn it into a prediction of what will happen at your company. When two measurements disagree, explain that they may not be comparable before choosing the one that confirms your idea.

## Concrete Example

Fictional headline: “An AI assistant now does the work of an entire team.” When you open the study, you discover that it measured a repetitive task with a small group and under supervision. A supportable claim might be “in this specific test, the system completed more steps than the previous version.” It does not follow that it can replace a team in production: sustained quality, total cost, exceptions, security, review time, and effects on people are all missing. Narrowing the headline to what was measured is not pessimism; it is precision.

## Guided Practice — Step-by-Step

1. Choose a public claim about AI. Save the headline, date, and link without treating a social media summary as the final source.
2. Find the cited study, report, or documentation and locate the method or table that supports the headline.
3. Note who participated, what task was measured, what comparison was used, how many cases were included, and which limitations the authors state.
4. If the headline announces a tool, also look for its official status page or documentation and check that the feature is still available on the date you consult it.
5. Write two sentences: one with the observed result and one with what it still does not allow you to conclude. Compare it with an independent source only if the metric matches.

This recipe is for critical reading, not for declaring which provider is “the best.” Your own evaluation should use permitted tasks, expected answers, and a version or date record you can explain.

## Validation and Troubleshooting

Check that you can get from the headline to the primary evidence. If you can find only a press release, label the figure as reported by the organization, not as independently verified. If the denominator is unclear, do not repeat the percentage. When a new edition changes the metric, dataset, or service, do not compare it directly with previous years without reviewing the method and product status.

## Common Mistakes

- Turning a controlled test into a claim about every job.
- Comparing benchmark scores with different scales, datasets, or dates.
- Confusing a product demo with an independent evaluation.
- Repeating a striking figure or an old tutorial without checking the primary source.

## In Summary

A headline starts an investigation; it does not end one. Follow the claim to its evidence, identify the task and date, and write a conclusion that does not exceed what was measured. Also confirm that the tool still exists: old documentation is not a promise of current availability.
