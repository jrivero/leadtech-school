## The idea is not a business yet

A technical idea describes something you could build; a business model explains who gets value, how they receive it, and what resources or revenue sustain the solution. You do not need to form a company to learn to think about these relationships. The goal of this workshop is to distinguish facts from assumptions and design low-cost tests, not to promise income or guarantee that an idea will find a market.

Strategyzer's Business Model Canvas organizes hypotheses about customer segments, value propositions, channels, relationships, activities, resources, partners, costs, and revenue. Its creators present it as a description to be revised in light of evidence, not as a decision proven by filling in boxes. The person who uses the product and the person who decides whether to pay for it may be different; write them down separately.

## Example: finding an association's procedures

Imagine an assistant that searches the internal documents of small associations and returns the source paragraph. The proposal is not “have AI,” but to reduce the time a volunteer coordinator spends finding the correct version of a procedure. The current alternative might be searching shared folders, asking another person, or reviewing old emails. That alternative also addresses some of the problem and has a switching cost.

Form specific hypotheses: “In associations with volunteer staff, someone responsible looks up instructions at least once a week”; “an answer with a document reference is more useful than an uncited generated answer”; “the organization can authorize a test using non-sensitive documents.” Record each statement as an assumption, available evidence, and confidence level. Do not treat a friendly response as proof that someone will pay.

## Design an experiment before investing

Choose the riskiest hypothesis—the one that, if false, would make the project no longer worthwhile. To investigate the need, interview five people responsible for organizations who are not just your friends. Ask about the last time they looked for a procedure: what they did, how long it took, what went wrong, and how they resolved it. Do not present your solution until you understand their current situation.

After that, you can manually simulate the flow with a public or fictional document: receive three questions, find the paragraph that would answer each, and show the reference. Measure how many questions have a clear source, which ones cannot be resolved, and what the person does next. Do not upload internal documents without authorization. If the value depends on confidential material, start with invented data and agree on permissions before any pilot.

Define a decision rule in advance so you do not change it after seeing the results. For example: continue to a limited pilot if at least three of the five people describe a recent problem and two agree to try a flow with approved documents. That threshold only guides this small experiment; it does not prove the size of the market or a real willingness to pay. A separate commercial test would need to observe a specific behavior and comply with the organization's rules.

## Step-by-step practice

1. Write down the user segment and, if different, the person who decides on the budget.
2. Note the work they are trying to do and the current alternative, not just the product you imagine.
3. Write three hypotheses about the need, value, and access to data.
4. Mark the riskiest hypothesis and design an interview or manual demonstration to test it.
5. Define in advance what observation would make you continue, change direction, or stop.
6. Record results separately from your interpretations and update the canvas version.

## Review criteria and common mistakes

The exercise is complete if each relevant part of the model appears as a revisable hypothesis, the experiment addresses the main question, and there is observable evidence in addition to opinions. If people cannot remember a recent example, ask about the actual process instead of arguing why they should like your solution. If the cost of operating the service is not yet known, label it as unknown rather than zero.

Do not build a complete application to avoid talking with users. Do not use “Would you like this?” as your only signal. Do not present a revenue projection as a proven forecast, and do not collect private documents for an informal demonstration. The canvas organizes questions; it does not replace financial advice or legal analysis when those are needed.

## Summary

Describe whom you help, what work they need to do, which alternative they use, and how you would deliver value sustainably. Treat the model as a set of hypotheses, run small experiments, and let evidence change the idea. A useful outcome may be to continue, adjust the segment, or decide not to build.
