## Prioritizing means deciding what not to do yet

When everything seems urgent, switching tasks often can create a sense of activity without bringing you closer to an outcome. Prioritizing means ordering work according to a specific objective and the information available, not promising that nothing will change. In this exercise, you will use one week as a planning horizon, but you do not need to implement a whole framework to practice. The three previous projects leave useful artifacts—brief, contract, tests, and constraints. Use them to make the real work visible, not to inflate the list. An AI assistant can suggest subtasks or identify dependencies, but you must check the assumptions and choose the order based on the objective, capacity, and new evidence.

Imagine that a local club is organizing workshop registrations and several people say they do not know whether their form was submitted. The list includes: show a confirmation after submission, validate required fields, review the colors, add an animation, and check the form using a keyboard. Before ordering the work, ask what the person needs to accomplish: submit the registration and know what happened.

## Exercise: from backlog to a plan

1. **Write small outcomes.** Turn each backlog item into an action that can be completed and checked. “Improve the form” is too broad; “show a confirmation message after accepting valid data” allows for a clear test. Keep a visible list, without hiding tasks in conversations or scattered notes.
2. **Compare using explicit criteria.** For each item, note its value to the user, the risk of postponing it, dependencies, and approximate effort (small, medium, or large). Do not add the numbers as though they were an objective measurement: they help make your reasoning visible. Validation can come before the confirmation message; an animation adds little if you still do not know whether submission worked.
3. **Set an objective.** Write an outcome statement for the cycle: “By the end, anyone who completes the form correctly receives a clear confirmation.” Choose only the work that fits your actual capacity; leave room to review, fix issues, and handle interruptions. It is better to finish a few verifiable tasks than to start many at once.
4. **Define acceptance before starting.** For the confirmation, you could require that it appear after a valid submission, not appear after an invalid one, and tell the person what to do next. Add a manual keyboard test if the form is operated on the web. A verifiable criterion reduces disagreements about what “done” means.
5. **Review at the end.** Compare the outcome with the objective and criteria; record what remains and why. Then note one process observation, such as breaking down large tasks earlier or reserving time for testing. Choose one change you can try in the next cycle and check whether it helped.

## What Scrum contributes—and what it does not

The Scrum Guide defines a framework for teams working on complex problems; it is not another name for any weekly list. In Scrum, planning establishes why the Sprint is valuable, what work can contribute to the objective, and how to approach it. The Daily Scrum is for developers to inspect progress toward the objective and adapt the plan, not for individuals to report their status. The review inspects the outcome with relevant participants, and the retrospective looks for ways to improve how the team works.

You can use those ideas to organize individual practice, but a list and a weekly meeting do not, by themselves, turn one person into a Scrum team. In this exercise, the written objective and criteria matter more than imitating ceremonies. If you work with a team, agree on who sets priorities, what capacity is available, and how a change will be communicated so the plan is not silently disrupted.

## Deliverable and common mistakes

Save an ordered list of at least five tasks, the cycle objective, acceptance criteria for two items, and a final review with one change you will try next. Another person should be able to understand why you chose the first tasks and repeat your checks. In the club example, validating entries and confirming submission come before the animation; checking keyboard access may become a higher priority if you discover that it blocks a registration.

Avoid labeling everything urgent, estimating with false precision, filling 100% of your time, or changing priorities without explaining what new information appeared. Do not measure progress by the number of cards started. This method is a planning practice: priorities are reviewed when the objective changes, a risk appears, or new evidence arrives. It does not guarantee results or replace the team's judgment.
