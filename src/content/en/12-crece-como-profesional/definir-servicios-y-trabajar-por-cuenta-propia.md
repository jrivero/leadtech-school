## Start with a need, not a promise

A professional proposal also starts with a problem and relies on evidence. Reuse projects from your portfolio: explain what need you addressed, how you tested the outcome, and what limits you kept. Experience with AI can help you shape an offer, but it does not replace interviews, verifiable quality, or a clear conversation about scope.

Working independently can start with a specific question: Who has a problem you know how to help solve, and how do they solve it today? A technical skill alone does not define a service. “I build websites” is broad; “I review the accessibility of a small website and deliver a prioritized list of barriers with agreed corrections” better describes who it serves and what they might receive. It does not guarantee sales or income: it is a hypothesis you need to test.

Market research means gathering signals before investing a lot of time. The U.S. Small Business Administration's guide can serve as a framework for questions, not as regulations for your country. Talk with people who resemble those you want to help. Ask what they are trying to do, where they get stuck, what solution they use now, how often it happens, and what would need to be true for them to consider the problem solved. Listen for recent examples; do not ask only whether your idea “sounds good.”

## Validation exercise

Choose a small, specific group—for example, local associations whose websites are maintained by volunteers—and conduct five exploratory conversations, if you can reach them without pressuring anyone. Keep a table with: profile, problem mentioned, recent example, current solution, consequence, and unanswered questions. Do not collect unnecessary personal data or draw conclusions about an entire market from a small sample.

Look for patterns and counterexamples. If several people independently describe a similar difficulty and already spend time solving it, that is a signal to investigate further, not proof that they would pay. If there is no urgency or no one wants to try a solution, change the hypothesis or stop pursuing the idea. A useful test could be a follow-up interview or a limited pilot with permission, not building a complete platform in advance.

## From interest to a well-defined service

Write a one-page outline before sending a proposal:

```text
Para quién:
Problema y situación en que aparece:
Resultado que ofrezco:
Entregables concretos:
Qué queda fuera:
Qué necesito de la persona cliente:
Cómo sabremos que está terminado:
Supuestos, revisiones y soporte posterior:
Próximo paso para validar la propuesta:
```

Example: an audit of an informational page, delivery of prioritized findings, and fixes for up to two agreed problems; it does not include a full redesign, indefinite maintenance, or guarantees about commercial results. Define how changes in scope will be requested and when the included support ends. If a new request changes the objective, pause and agree to the change in writing before continuing.

## Pilot proposal and boundaries

Start with a test you can complete and verify using existing resources: a discovery conversation, a short audit, or a non-production prototype. Describe the outcome and acceptance criteria in plain language. Estimate the time for the work, coordination, reviews, and learning internally; note uncertainties that could increase the effort. There is no universal rate or formula that guarantees profitability. Check your assumptions with people in the field and review the costs or licenses of any tool before including it; the service should not depend on a paid subscription the client has not approved.

If you work with another person's data or systems, agree on what information you may access, how it will be shared, and who will approve each change. Use free or local tools only when appropriate and authorized. Do not promise timelines, savings, or sales that you do not control. Explain dependencies, risks, and what you cannot guarantee.

## Requirements and common mistakes

Tax, contract, privacy, intellectual-property, and registration obligations depend on location and activity. This lesson is not legal or financial advice: before accepting work, check current information from local institutional sources and consult qualified professionals if you have questions about your situation. The SBA provides planning and research material for the United States; it does not replace those checks in another jurisdiction.

Common mistakes include offering “anything with AI,” building before talking with potential users, accepting undefined work, omitting who will provide content and access, or confusing friendly interest with proven demand. Keep your notes, record which hypothesis changed, and choose the next step based on evidence. Sometimes the most useful validation outcome is to narrow the service, look for a different audience, or not pursue the idea for now.
